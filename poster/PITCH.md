# Questions you may get

## Method and numbers

**Is 84 people not a very small sample?**
84 participants but 488 coded sessions, and it is a depth design, not a
prevalence estimate. It characterises *how* people use each tool, not what
percentage of the country does what.

**What is Prolific?**
An online platform where people sign up to be paid research participants and
researchers recruit against screening filters. Here: US adults who already used
both tools, on desktop so they could open their own history. Fast and screened,
but a convenience sample, which is our first limitation.

**Why 488 sessions and not 500?**
Twelve were dropped in quality control: answers that were incomplete or
uninformative, for example "cannot remember", and answers that looked
machine-generated, unusually long or oddly polished.

**How did they decide which task a session was?**
Two researchers read the whole session, not just the query: the prompt, the
stated goal, what triggered it, what the person did, and what they did
afterwards. Lookup and exploratory come from established search literature,
guidance from prior work, and they added "generative" because nothing covered
asking a tool to produce something. They piloted on 20 sessions, then coded a
subset independently and measured agreement: Cohen's kappa 0.83 to 0.96.

**What is Cohen's kappa?**
Agreement between two coders after subtracting the agreement you would get by
chance. 0 is guessing, 1 is perfect, above 0.8 is strong. It is how they show the
categories were defined clearly enough to be applied consistently.

**What does χ² = 84.6, p < .001 mean?**
Chi-square measures how far the observed table sits from what you would see if
the tool made no difference to the task. With three degrees of freedom anything
above 7.8 is significant, so 84.6 is enormous. The p value is the chance of a gap
this big arising from sampling alone: under one in a thousand. It says the two
distributions genuinely differ, not that the difference is large.

**What is the "r = +3.2" on the topics result?**
A standardised residual. Chi-square says something in the table differs;
residuals say which cell drives it. Beyond plus or minus 2 counts. Shopping is
+3.2 on search, self and social skills +5.7 on chatbots, the largest single
effect in the study. Exploratory is 0.77, which is why we call that a tie.

**What counts as a "session"?**
For search, a series of interactions aimed at a single information need, the
standard definition from Jansen and colleagues. For a chatbot, one continuous
conversation on one task or topic.

**Is self-reported data not unreliable?**
That is the risk the design attacks: participants reflect on sessions they are
looking at in their own history, not sessions they are recalling. The authors
still list reconstruction effects as a limitation, and so do we.

## Scope and currency

**February 2025 is old. Is this still true?**
Deliberately a pre-AI-Mode baseline, taken while the tools were still separable.
Everything that has converged since is measured against a snapshot like this one.
Whether the gap has closed is exactly our closing question.

**What is AI Mode?**
Google's conversational search mode, rolled out after this data was collected.
It, and AI Overviews, are the convergence the paper is a baseline for.

**US participants only. Does it generalise?**
Not beyond digitally literate US adults, and the authors say so. The tool mix
does match the US market at the time: Google around 87% of search, ChatGPT around
88% of chatbot use, which is why "search engine" and "chatbot" work as umbrella
terms here.

## Interpretation

**Why is asking a chatbot about health a problem?**
It is not, in itself. The finding is the mismatch: people justify chatbots on
convenience, then use them where reliability matters most, in an interface that
offers no source and no confidence signal, and they act on the answer.

**Did they check whether the chatbot answers were actually wrong?**
No, and this is worth being straight about. The study measures behaviour and
reasoning, not answer accuracy. It shows people bring high-stakes questions to a
tool that gives them no way to verify the reply. Whether those replies were
correct is a different study.

**Chatbots cite sources now. Does that kill the finding?**
It is the right challenge, and it is our closing question. Citations existing is
not the same as people using them, and prior work already finds users
over-relying on fluent answers without checking. The behavioural baseline here is
what you would measure the change against.

**Is exploratory really a tie?**
Yes, 31.0% against 27.9%, residual 0.77, not significant. Open-ended learning is
the one job where these tools already substitute for each other.

## The paper and the poster

**Are those real screenshots?**
No, and we say so on the poster. They are our illustrations of the findings, laid
out in each tool's idiom. The queries in quotation marks are real participant
examples quoted in the paper.

**What were the research questions?**
RQ1, what patterns emerge in how users choose between the two. RQ2, how sessions
and task contexts differ. RQ3, how stated preferences align with actual usage.
The two panes answer RQ1 and RQ2; the black band is RQ3.

**What is new here compared with earlier work?**
Earlier comparisons used lab tasks or interaction logs, which capture what people
did but not why they chose that tool or what they expected. This pairs real
sessions with the user's own reasoning about them. The three contributions are
the two philosophies with their task profiles, the misalignment between stated
and actual use, and the evaluation implications.

**Where are the implications on the poster?**
Deliberately not on it. The poster carries the evidence; we supply the argument.
The right-hand column carries the limits and the question we want to discuss.

**What would you do next?**
The authors suggest less digitally literate users, combining donated log data
with reflections, and following whole information episodes across several tools
rather than single sessions. We would add: rerun it now that chatbots cite
sources, and see whether anyone clicks.
